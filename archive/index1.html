Act 1

Daring to build

Every age is remembered by what it built, with canals, railways, telephone lines, highways and internet cables each in turn transforming the way the world works.

The next age runs on electricity and it is arriving fast.

Its success depends on whether we build the infrastructure it runs on. That choice is being made now.

Transformative infrastructure

Infrastructure can change the world, and it can do so quickly.

Railways opened up the world with unprecedented speed:

stretching across the American plains, criss-crossing Europe and joining up continents.

Along the rails, towns and factories rose, turning local markets national.

Highways carried people and goods to every corner of new regions, from the Pan-American Highway to India's Golden Quadrilateral,

with new economies flourishing along the roadsides.

Cables and masts across every landscape and ocean

brought telecoms and the internet to the world,

moving information across the globe instantaneously

and transforming societies within a generation.

Each was built with speed and conviction.

Each made possible ways of living and kinds of growth that no one could have imagined before.

The same will be true with the great infrastructure build-out of the current age: modern grids.

Act 2

The world moved on – the grid did not

A world remade

Almost nothing in modern life resembles the world the first grids were built to serve.

How people work, travel, communicate and create has changed beyond recognition in the last fifty years.

And yet, the grid has stayed fundamentally the same.

The first grids linked centralised power stations to cities, households and businesses, turning electricity into a central part of our daily lives.

Since then, the grid has grown to connect new sources of power such as wind and solar. But the way it is planned, built and run has changed very little.

Grids are out of step with a world faster and hungrier for power than any before: the age of power.

Act 3

Building for the age of power

Electrotech

Across the world, countries are transforming themselves into electrostates:

economies that run on electricity from the ground up.

Cars, trains, kitchens, heating, and factories

are switching from expensive fossil fuels to clean electricity.

Countries that once imported their energy now make their own with domestic wind and solar.

AI, already reshaping how we live, runs in data centres: vast halls of machines that draw power around the clock.

The nations that build a grid for this age of power will be where AI, industry and investment go.

The modern grid

The modern grid works in a fundamentally different way

The old grid sent power one way, from a few large power stations to everyone else.

In a modern grid, power is generated everywhere – homes, farms and businesses can produce their own.

Power flows both ways. Homes and businesses draw electricity from the grid and send it back in.

Behind the scenes, smart devices do the work. Cars charge and heating runs when power is plentiful and cheap.

Batteries across the network store and release electricity when it is scarce. Bills fall and the system runs better.

Sensors let the grid see itself, and automated controls respond in seconds. Built this way, the system no longer depends on a few key plants, and becomes more resilient.

The wires are smarter too. Lines fitted with sensors carry more power when the weather allows, and better cables carry much more on the same towers.

The world needs more grid than ever, and every kilometre built or upgraded now carries far more.

Together, this means far more can connect to the grid we already have: new wind and solar, new homes, factories, businesses and data centres.

The connection queues holding back investment start to clear and projects get online quicker, without waiting for a new line to be built.

This is not the old grid made bigger – it is a modern grid, fit for the age of power.

Two paths, one way forward

For nations still building, this is a chance to lead.

Undersea cables can link Indonesia's islands together,

and villages across Africa and South Asia can install low-cost solar panels to draw power from the sun.

Just as Kenya embraced mobile without waiting for landlines, countries can jump straight to building for this age.

For nations with older grids, the task is not to start over but to transform outdated systems.
